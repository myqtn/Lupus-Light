About Lupus Light

What Is Lupus?

Lupus is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system mistakenly attacks healthy tissue throughout the body. This causes widespread inflammation and can affect the joints, skin, kidneys, heart, lungs, and brain. The most common form is Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE).

Lupus affects approximately 1.5 million people in the United States and at least five million worldwide. It most commonly affects women of childbearing age, and women of colour are diagnosed at higher rates. Because symptoms often mimic other conditions, lupus is sometimes called "the great imitator" — diagnosis can take years.

There is currently no cure for lupus, but with proper medical treatment, most people can lead full, active lives. Management typically involves medications prescribed by a rheumatologist, regular monitoring, and lifestyle adjustments to reduce symptom activity.

Common Symptoms of Lupus

Lupus is highly individual. Common symptoms include:

  • Fatigue — extreme, debilitating tiredness that does not improve with rest
  • Joint pain and swelling — particularly in the hands, wrists, and knees
  • Butterfly rash — a rash across the cheeks and nose
  • Skin sensitivity — photosensitivity and rashes worsened by sun exposure
  • Brain fog — difficulty with memory, concentration, and clear thinking
  • Hair loss — thinning hair or patches of hair loss
  • Chest pain — from inflammation around the heart or lungs
  • Mood changes — depression, anxiety, and emotional fluctuations are common

Symptoms often come and go in periods called flares (when symptoms worsen) and remissions (when symptoms improve). Tracking this pattern over time is one of the most valuable things a person with lupus can do.

Why Symptom Tracking Matters

Symptom tracking is one of the most evidence-supported self-management strategies for people with chronic illnesses. Patients who keep detailed records of their symptoms, sleep, energy, mood, and lifestyle factors are better able to: identify personal triggers, detect early warning signs, have more productive conversations with their healthcare providers, and feel a greater sense of control over their own health.

Lupus Light makes this process as simple as possible. With daily check-ins that take less than two minutes, you can build a meaningful record of your wellness journey — one that you own entirely and can share with your doctor at any time.

How Wellness Journaling Helps

Beyond symptom scores and sliders, keeping a personal journal has well-documented psychological benefits. Writing about your experiences, emotions, and daily challenges can reduce stress and anxiety, help you process the emotional impact of chronic illness, and document moments of improvement and resilience — which can be motivating on hard days.

The journal feature in Lupus Light is completely private — it stays on your device and is never transmitted anywhere.

Lupus Light was created as a free, accessible, educational wellness tool for people living with lupus and other autoimmune conditions. Our goal is to empower you with better awareness of your own wellness patterns — not to replace your medical care team, but to complement it.

Healthy Lifestyle Habits That Support Wellness

The following general wellness habits are widely recognised as supportive for people with autoimmune conditions. Always discuss any lifestyle changes with your healthcare provider:

  • Prioritise rest: Sleep is critical for immune function. Listen to your body, especially during periods of higher symptom activity.
  • Sun protection: Many people with lupus are photosensitive. Use high-SPF sunscreen daily and wear protective clothing.
  • Stress management: Mindfulness, meditation, gentle breathing, and relaxation practices may help support your nervous system.
  • Gentle exercise: Low-impact activities such as walking, yoga, and swimming may help maintain mobility and mood. Always pace yourself.
  • Anti-inflammatory nutrition: A balanced diet rich in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, and omega-3 fatty acids is generally supportive.
  • Hydration: Staying well-hydrated supports kidney health and overall wellbeing.
  • Social connection: Support groups for lupus patients — in person or online — can provide emotional support and practical advice.
  • Medication adherence: Never skip or stop prescribed medications without consulting your doctor.

Article 1: Understanding Lupus Symptoms and Flares

One of the most challenging aspects of living with lupus is its unpredictability. Lupus moves in cycles — periods of increased disease activity called flares, followed by periods of relative stability called remissions. Understanding this pattern is the foundation of effective self-management.

A lupus flare can be triggered by many factors. Sun exposure is one of the most well-documented: ultraviolet light can stimulate the immune system and cause skin rashes, joint pain, and systemic inflammation. This is why sun protection is not optional for most people with lupus — it is a daily necessity.

Stress — both physical and emotional — is another major trigger. The stress response activates hormones that can dysregulate immune function. Major life events, illness, surgery, and even positive but overwhelming experiences can all precede increased symptom activity.

Infections are also common triggers. Because the immune system is already dysregulated in lupus, any infection that activates it can spill over into increased disease activity. Staying up to date with vaccinations and seeking prompt treatment for infections is important.

Sleep deprivation is increasingly recognised as both a trigger and a consequence of lupus activity. Poor sleep impairs immune regulation, increases inflammatory markers, and worsens pain sensitivity and mood — all of which can compound lupus symptoms. Prioritising sleep hygiene is therefore an active part of wellness management.

Recognising the early signs of a flare is equally important. For many people, subtle warning signs appear days before a full flare: increased fatigue, morning stiffness, low-grade fever, or mood changes. Learning to recognise your personal early warning signs allows you to take protective action sooner.

Important: If you believe you are experiencing a flare, always contact your healthcare provider. Early intervention often leads to better outcomes. Do not attempt to manage a flare based on app information alone.

Article 2: Managing Fatigue and Daily Energy

Fatigue is the single most commonly reported symptom in lupus. Unlike ordinary tiredness that improves with rest, lupus fatigue can be profound, relentless, and disproportionate to the amount of activity performed. It is a physiological symptom of the disease, not a sign of weakness.

The fatigue in lupus has multiple causes: active inflammation consumes enormous energy, anaemia reduces oxygen to cells, pain disrupts sleep, and medications may cause drowsiness. Depression — which affects a significant proportion of lupus patients — is itself exhausting. All of these factors can overlap and compound each other.

The Pacing Strategy

One of the most effective evidence-based approaches to managing energy with a chronic illness is energy pacing — living within your energy envelope rather than borrowing from tomorrow. Practical strategies include: breaking tasks into smaller steps with rest in between, prioritising important activities for your peak-energy times of day, learning to delegate and ask for help, and planning recovery time after demanding activities.

Sleep Hygiene for Lupus

Because sleep is so central to immune regulation, improving sleep quality is often one of the highest-leverage interventions available. Evidence-supported sleep hygiene practices include: maintaining a consistent sleep-wake schedule, creating a cool and dark sleep environment, avoiding screens for 60 minutes before bed, limiting caffeine after midday, and using relaxation techniques to prepare for sleep.

Nutrition and Energy

What you eat profoundly affects your energy levels and inflammatory load. While there is no specific "lupus diet," many specialists recommend an anti-inflammatory eating pattern: emphasising vegetables, fruits, whole grains, lean proteins, and healthy fats, while reducing ultra-processed foods and refined sugars. Always discuss dietary changes with your healthcare provider.

Article 3: The Importance of Tracking Wellness Patterns

Self-monitoring has been consistently shown in medical research to improve outcomes in chronic disease management. For lupus specifically, the value of systematic symptom tracking cannot be overstated.

Building a Clinical Picture Over Time

No single doctor's appointment captures the full complexity of living with lupus. Appointments are brief, and patients frequently feel pressure that makes it hard to recall their experience accurately. A detailed symptom record changes this dynamic. When you arrive at an appointment with months of data showing your pain, fatigue, sleep quality, mood, and energy trends, you give your doctor a much richer clinical picture.

Detecting Early Symptom Changes

If you are tracking daily, you can see subtle changes emerge in your data — a gradual increase in fatigue scores, worsening sleep quality, declining mood — before they become a full flare. This early detection window allows you to rest more, avoid known triggers, and contact your doctor earlier.

Motivating Positive Behaviour

There is also a motivational element to tracking. Seeing your consistency streak grow, noticing that your wellness score improved after a week of better sleep and reduced stress, or simply feeling a sense of agency over your own data — these things have real psychological value. Chronic illness can feel out of control. Systematic tracking is one concrete way to reclaim a sense of participation in your own care.

Article 4: Stress Management and Mindfulness for Autoimmune Wellness

The relationship between stress and autoimmune disease is bidirectional and well-documented. Chronic psychological stress activates hormonal pathways that can lead to immune imbalance and — in people with underlying autoimmune conditions — potentially increased disease activity. The majority of people with lupus report that stress is one of their most consistent personal triggers.

Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction

Mindfulness — the practice of deliberately directing attention to the present moment without judgement — has been studied in chronic illness populations for decades. Research in lupus and rheumatoid arthritis populations has found that mindfulness-based interventions can reduce perceived stress, improve sleep quality, reduce depression and anxiety, and improve overall quality of life. Even 10–15 minutes of guided meditation or mindful breathing, practised consistently, has measurable benefits over time.

Box Breathing

Box breathing (inhale for 4 counts, hold for 4 counts, exhale for 4 counts, hold for 4 counts) activates the parasympathetic nervous system and reduces the acute stress response. Try the Box Breathing tool in the Meditate section for a guided experience.

The Role of Sound in Relaxation

Nature sounds (rain, ocean, forest) have been shown to reduce cortisol levels, lower heart rate, and improve mood in multiple studies. Binaural beats — audio patterns where slightly different frequencies are played in each ear — have been the subject of research suggesting potential benefits for relaxation. The Sound section of this app offers these tools for relaxation support. These are not medical treatments.

Seeking Professional Support

If you are struggling with the psychological burden of lupus, speaking to a mental health professional who has experience with chronic illness can be transformative. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) are both well-supported approaches. You do not have to manage the emotional weight of this condition alone.

Lupus Light is committed to protecting your privacy. This policy explains how your information is handled when you use Lupus Light.

1. Data Storage

Lupus Light stores all data exclusively on your own device using your browser's local storage. This includes your name, daily wellness entries, streak, achievements, and preferences. None of this data is transmitted to any server, cloud, or third party. We have no access to your data whatsoever.

2. No Medical Records Transmitted

Lupus Light does not collect, process, or store medical records. The symptom data you enter is for personal wellness awareness only, stored locally on your device. It is not shared with healthcare providers, insurers, employers, or any third party.

3. Local Storage

This application uses your browser's built-in localStorage API. Your data persists between sessions on the same device and browser. Clearing your browser data will erase your saved wellness data. Your data is not accessible from other devices unless you export it.

4. Third-Party Services

This app loads Chart.js from cdn.jsdelivr.net (a static library — no data transmitted). We do not use Google Analytics, Facebook Pixel, or any other tracking scripts.

5. Your Rights

You can view your data via the export functions, delete it by clearing browser local storage, and export it at any time for use with your healthcare provider.

6. Children's Privacy

Lupus Light is not directed at children under 13 years of age. We do not knowingly collect information from children.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my data private? ▼
Yes — completely. All your data is stored only on your own device. Nothing is sent to any server. We have no access to your health data.
Can I use this instead of seeing my doctor? ▼
No. Lupus Light is an educational wellness tracking tool only. It cannot replace professional medical care. Always consult your rheumatologist or doctor for medical concerns.
Will my data be lost if I clear my browser? ▼
Yes. Since data is stored in browser local storage, clearing browser data will erase your saved entries. Export your data regularly using the Doctor Report feature to keep backups.
What does the wellness score mean? ▼
The wellness score is a personal awareness indicator only. It has no clinical meaning and should not be interpreted as a medical measurement.
Is the app free? ▼
